THE CREATIVE PREMISE

An artist has a way of seeing. A designer develops its form, and a producer realises the work through materials and making. A custodian recognises something of themselves in it and brings their own meaning through wearing it. Hewge finds the connection, commissions the work and makes its authorship legible at the agreed reveal stages.

The useful creative question is: what could this piece let someone express that they have not found in anything they already own?

That expression could be quiet or conspicuous, personal or social, playful or exacting. We discover it through the work and conversations with possible wearers. We do not settle it by choosing an age band or a city. Nor must every piece appeal to the same person. Hewge's continuity comes from the quality of its choices, its standard of making and the way it presents and supports each original.

DRAFT BRIEF FOR A COLLABORATOR

Hewge commissions original works to be worn. Each piece is made once and presented with the people and decisions behind it. We are interested in a work that someone feels a personal connection to and wants to live in.

We want to explore what you could make here that you have not had the opportunity to realise elsewhere. Bring your own visual language, approach to form or expertise in making. The collaboration should produce something that benefits from the particular people involved.

Together we will develop the idea, consider how it meets a body, test its materials and construction, and refine it into a finished original. We will agree the scope, budget, responsibilities and review stages before commissioned development begins. We will also agree what can be shared from the process, when identities and details are revealed, and how every contributor is credited.

The piece will have a name, a documented identity and a dedicated editorial story. We would like to record a conversation with you about your work and this collaboration, with agreed editing and publication arrangements. An annual purchasable coffee-table book will bring together the year's pieces and their making in greater depth than the website. Agree the print uses separately from online publication. Our intention is for a custodian to understand the choices that make this particular piece meaningful, and for others to discover the people behind it.

To begin, show us work that matters to you. Tell us what you want to explore, what would make this collaboration worthwhile, and what you need from us to do it well.

End of draft brief. Fill in the actual commission, fees and deliverables before using it as a project offer. It has not been sent to anyone.

CURATION AND PAIRING

For the first pieces, the recommendation is direct human curation. Build understanding of a small set of practices through existing work and conversation. Choose a promising collaboration for a reason that can be expressed plainly. Invitations and recommendations can gradually create a broader pool without requiring application software or a formal competition.

Consider:
- A distinctive point of view visible in existing work.
- The quality of their craft, or a clear understanding of the expertise they need alongside them.
- An idea that becomes richer when translated into something worn.
- Curiosity about another discipline and the ability to discuss and revise a shared work.
- A realistic route to making the piece within an agreed budget.

The story can be about close observation, a material obsession, a place, a technique or a persistent question. A dramatic biography, fame and a fluent on-camera persona are not prerequisites. Let people describe their own practice. Credit a single person for multiple roles when that is what actually happened.

A ballot or lottery for collaborators remains only a possible future idea. There is no selection mechanism to implement now. This is separate from the established highest-valid-bid auction. Similarly, being chosen as a gifted recipient is distinct from buying through that auction; describe each relationship accurately.

WHAT COLLABORATORS SHOULD GET

Make the opportunity substantive: agreed compensation; the chance to realise a strong work; clear authorship; good photographs and a case study; approved assets they can share; and a lasting route from the piece's story to their own practice. Decide which outputs Hewge funds and produces in the actual agreement.

Artist, designer and producer should receive clear credit at the stages agreed in the release's reveal record. A deliberately later identity reveal must still lead to accurate attribution. The custodian should be able to identify the contributions once revealed. Interest in a collaborator's other work should have somewhere useful to go, with their permission. A well-documented case study and agreed book treatment give them a substantial record for future opportunities. Later career developments can become part of the piece's history when real and relevant.

Hewge seeks recognition that matters within the work's intended audience. Influential and consequential people's choices, wearing, recommendations and attention can contribute to that social proof and to discovery of the collaborators. No specific audience is fixed, and recognition or career outcomes are not guaranteed compensation for commissioned work.

Hewge supplies curation, editorial work, production coordination, presentation and the route to an owner. Social participation and image-use permissions are agreed specifically; they are not unlimited obligations attached to a commission. Paid work should stand on its own even if the release gets little attention.

DEVELOPING THE WORK TOGETHER

Begin with existing portfolios and a conversation. Bespoke design development is then a defined paid stage, with an agreed stopping point. Avoid commissioning the whole production before understanding whether the collaboration works.

Use a simple sequence, adjusted to the actual project:

Discovery: understand the practice, possible wearing experiences and why these collaborators belong together. Capture a short shared intention, the questions to explore and a provisional budget. A named recipient may inform a gift; an auction piece must work without pretending its eventual owner has already been chosen.

Concept: develop the visual and physical idea together. Ask what makes it recognisable, what changes in movement, what a wearer might connect with and which material decisions carry the meaning. Agree a direction and the tests it needs.

Development: make the necessary material and fit samples. Look at the work on a body, not only as an image. Review comfort, movement, finish, care and whether the original intention survives the physical result. Sampling does not create repeat saleable editions.

Refinement: record what is working and what needs to change. Make feedback specific: identify the observed problem, connect it to the shared intention, and ask for a response. For example, if the composition disappears when the piece moves, explore that with the designer rather than asking vaguely for more impact.

Completion: approve the physical piece, true measurements, condition, credits, care guidance, imagery and story as one coherent presentation. Resolve the budget and launch prerequisites before accepting bids.

At each review, the options are to proceed, revise a defined part, or stop. Agree the included review rounds and additional-work terms for each commission. Changed scope needs a new cost/time agreement. Pay for completed agreed work if the project stops, according to the agreement. Hewge can decline to release a piece; it should not leave collaborators working indefinitely for an uncertain outcome. The artist and designer retain room to propose a better solution than the founder's first idea.

THE RECORDED CONVERSATION

Treat the interview as part of discovering and documenting the work. Jez's conversation with the collaborators can be the source for the editorial page and, if the material warrants it, an audio or film edition. A recurring podcast, fixed duration and elaborate studio are not prerequisites. Begin with clear sound, attentive questions and enough time to follow an unexpected answer.

Before recording, agree the purpose, recording format, intended uses, off-record boundaries and review process, including website extracts and deeper book material. Share the proposed edit and quotations for review before publication. These editorial recommendations draw on the Oral History Association's interview guidance [1]. Store original recordings and permissions privately with a defined access/retention arrangement. Publish only approved material; consent to an interview is not permission to release every raw file or private commissioning discussion.

Useful prompts, adapted to the actual person:
- Which work of yours should we begin with, and what were you trying to resolve in it?
- What do you keep returning to in your practice?
- What have you wanted to make but not yet had the opportunity to realise?
- What changes when your work is worn and moves with someone?
- What did your collaborator see that you had missed?
- Show us a decision you changed. What made the earlier version insufficient?
- Which small detail would you want the owner to notice?
- What should remain open for the wearer to interpret?

Ask for a concrete example or demonstration when an answer becomes abstract. Return during making and after completion when there is something new to discuss. Check names, credits and factual claims. Preserve the contributor's meaning in edits. Use transcripts and captions so the story is available without listening, and let the finished content determine its length.

HOW THE STORY REACHES THE OWNER

Give the work different depths of entry, arranged to suit the release. A first encounter might be the piece or an approved detail. The introduction and editorial page develop its identity, contributors and decisions according to the reveal plan. A recording lets an interested person spend time with its makers. The annual book adds a fuller account of the year's works and their making. The person chooses how deeply to engage.

The eventual conversational story should be easy to remember: who made it, what they explored together, and a detail the wearer connects with. We are not writing a sales script for the owner. They should be free to say what it means to them. If the story only works after memorising a long explanation, refine the presentation.

Connect each claim to something observable: a process image, a maker's explanation, the construction, the composition in motion or a detail in the finished work. Hewge administrators design the mystery separately for every release, including identities and material details that may appear later. Record the sequence, disclosure obligations and contributor publication timing before release. The current application requires artist/material by binding auction opening; plans beyond that need the corresponding review and implementation before use. See docs/administrator-playbook.md.

THE BOOK MATERIAL

The annual book covers the year's pieces and how they came to be, with greater depth than the website and an exceptionally desirable physical presentation. Gather high-resolution imagery, fuller interviews, samples and development records during the commission, with explicit print rights, credits and agreed reveal timing. Deeper content means richer creative understanding, not publishing the internal journey playbook.

Include approved artistic and making decisions. Exclude internal administration and influence strategy, collaborator selection/evaluation records, fees and contracts, customer/transaction journeys and authentication/security procedures. Private or off-record material stays private; only specifically cleared excerpts enter the book. These are editorial boundaries even when a raw recording contains both kinds of material.

Complimentary copies for custodians, potentially signed by one or several artists, are possible future features. Participation, compensation where applicable and logistics for signing need separate agreement. Neither complimentary copies nor signatures are current obligations of the commission.

THE COST REVIEW

A successful piece must both deserve its price and support the people who made it. Establish costs during development, before the final work and public opening bid are committed.

The private cost review should include artist and designer fees; pattern and sample work; materials; making; fitting and testing; the agreed editorial/photography work; packaging and certificate; insurance and fulfilment; payment costs; relevant tax treatment; allocated operating time; an appropriate contingency; and any defined aftercare commitment. Include any sales-linked collaborator payment in the variable costs. Allocate a founding gift explicitly if later sales are expected to fund it, rather than losing that cost between projects.

At the proposed opening bid, calculate what Hewge actually retains after payment fees, applicable tax and any other amounts it must pass on, together with separately charged delivery income/costs. Compare that with the fully costed commission and an agreed contribution for continuing the business. Do not count the same expense twice. Use real quotes and confirmed terms, not a projected high winning bid. This is a planning review, not a tax calculation or an automated pricing feature.

If the lowest accepted price cannot support the commission, revise the scope or proposed price, or stop before committing more cost. If development is being subsidised, record that separately and explicitly. Fund agreed collaborator payments even if the piece receives no bids. A cost-covering opening bid does not guarantee a buyer. The first real price remains undecided; the existing CHF 339 prototype price has not been changed.

KEEP THE FIRST PROCESS SMALL

The first version needs a shared brief, a private commission folder, a cost record, clear responsibilities, a few agreed reviews and a compelling finished piece. It does not need a collaborator portal, automated ranking, ballot, lottery or a large content calendar.

For each commission, record who is contributing what; the current intention and approved direction; scope and budget; review decisions; actual material/fit information; credits and website/book publication permissions; the administrator's reveal record; approved editorial assets; and launch readiness. Raw recordings, contact details and contracts stay out of the repository. Its public trail contains only approved work and accurate authorship. Keep book-cleared creative material separate from internal operating records.

Current software has an artist biography and basic private cost fields. Separate designer/producer credits, linked collaborator profiles, recorded interviews, arbitrary reveal sequences and book ordering need deliberate content/flow design before being described as implemented. The working documents do not add those features or activate bidding. The commissioning and book-editorial process can be managed in private project records before building specialist software.

The immediate creative work is to discover distinctive practices, explore possible collaborations and test the brief in conversation. Let those encounters refine who the piece could speak to. Choose the first commission when the artistic proposition, ability to realise it and economics hold together.
