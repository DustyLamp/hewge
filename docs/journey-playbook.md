# Hewge journey playbook

The journey of a piece through the founder, designer, artist, piece producer, purchaser, custodian, repurchaser and influenced party. Each stage connects what happens, the context given to the person involved and the emotional experience. The unresolved handovers are collected at the end.

The **purchaser** pays. The **custodian** receives, wears and cares for the piece. The **repurchaser** acquires it later from an existing owner. The registered ownership record needs to follow the intended relationship between them. The **piece producer** is the person or team physically making and finishing the work; one contributor may also fulfil the artist or designer role.

The instructions below begin with purpose and the desired outcome, leaving room for the recipient's judgment. For the founder, they are prompts for decisions; for other participants, they are draft language Hewge can use at the relevant handover. Auction, payment, consent and ownership requirements remain explicit. Emotional experiences describe what the journey should make possible, rather than something a person is expected to feel.

This is the intended experience. The decisions still open below remain open; the journeys do not imply that every service or feature is already implemented. Illustrative locations, demographics, occupations and selection ideas do not define the audience or become requirements.

The [concept and administrator playbook](administrator-playbook.md) sets out the shared principles and each release's reveal process. Social proof includes the standing Hewge earns in society and its intended audience, especially through influential and consequential people whose judgment matters there. Purchases, gifts, real wear, recommendations and cultural recognition can all contribute; auction preference is never exchanged for influence.

The piece journeys below are joined by an annual purchasable coffee-table book covering the year's pieces and how they came to be, in greater depth than the website. Its internal participant touchpoints follow the eight journeys; these maps are not book content. The book has its own purchase route to develop and does not inherit garment auction or ownership-transfer requirements.

## The introductory video for artists and designers

The founder's video introduces the concept before an artist or designer enters a detailed project conversation. It gives them enough context to respond with their own ideas and questions.

The introduction covers:

- **What Hewge is bringing into the world:** original works to be worn, made with enough care that someone can form a lasting relationship with a piece.
- **Why each piece exists once:** its particular creators, artistic decisions and making are central to its identity.
- **What each contributor brings:** the artist's visual language, the designer's approach to form, and the producer's ability to realise the work physically. Roles can overlap.
- **What the wearer receives:** the physical work, an understanding of its creators and history, and room to express their own identity through it.
- **How the collaboration develops:** a shared intention, agreed scope, exploration, iteration and a considered finished piece. Interviews and process documentation make the work understandable to others.
- **What happens next:** a conversation about the collaborator's practice, what interests them and what they could imagine contributing. The commission-specific scope, compensation and responsibilities are agreed separately.

Context-led invitation:

> This introduces what we want to make possible through Hewge. Bring your own point of view to it. Show us work that matters to you, what you would like to explore, and what you would need from a collaboration to realise it well.

## The piece producer

| Stage | What happens | Context-led instruction | Emotional experience |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Understand the work | Receive the creative intention, the artist's and designer's contributions and the intended experience of wearing. Establish the producer's role in the collaboration. | Your making will determine how this intention feels in real life. Understand what matters to the work and bring your judgment about how it can be realised. | Inclusion, interest and recognition of their craft. |
| Assess feasibility | Examine materials, techniques, construction, specialist needs, timing and costs. Identify dependencies and uncertainties before they become production commitments. | We need to understand what the work requires physically. Show us where the proposed methods support the intention, where they create difficulties and what alternatives are available. | Confidence in being heard; responsibility for making the idea achievable. |
| Agree the production scope | Establish responsibility for sourcing, samples, fabrication, finishing, quality checks and handover. Agree costs, review points and how deviations are approved. | A clear scope protects the quality of the work and everyone's contribution. Confirm what you will deliver, what you depend on and which changes need a shared decision. | Clarity, security and readiness to commit. |
| Sample and resolve | Test the relevant materials, construction, fit, finish and behaviour in use with the designer and artist. Feed observations back into the developing piece. | Samples help us understand the physical consequences of the idea. Use them to expose what needs resolving and explain how each proposed adjustment affects the work. | Curiosity, problem-solving and satisfaction as the construction resolves. |
| Make the original | Produce the agreed physical piece, preserving the approved artistic and design intent. Record consequential changes and raise issues before an irreversible departure. | This is the original that someone will live with. Make it to the agreed intention and standard; bring back any issue that would materially change its form, artwork, fit or finish. | Concentration, care and responsibility for an irreplaceable result. |
| Inspect and complete | Examine the finished work against the agreed construction, measurements, finish and condition. Resolve defects and establish accurate care and handling information. | The wearer needs a piece they can trust in use. Check the physical result, make its care requirements clear and identify any limitation that affects wearing or handling. | Pride, scrutiny and relief when the work meets the agreed standard. |
| Hand over | Supply the finished piece and the agreed production, condition and care records. Coordinate protective handling, authentication attachment and presentation where these fall within the producer's scope. | The care in making must survive the handover. Give the next person the information and handling conditions needed to protect the piece and present it accurately. | Satisfaction and confidence that the work will be respected. |
| Support its continuing life | Where agreed, advise on fitting, repair, restoration or later interventions. Help distinguish normal wear from damage and document consequential work. | Future care needs to respect the original materials and construction. Explain the available options and their effects before an intervention is agreed. | Continuing pride and responsibility, supported by clear limits on the commitment. |

The producer's contribution is part of the piece's authorship and history. Technical observations need a route back to the artist, designer and founder while decisions can still be made together.

Three distinct questions need answers: **is the piece authentic, is the seller entitled to transfer it, and does its current condition match what was offered?**

For subsequent gifts, the transfer-fee basis is the last verified sale value. Initial gift registration and handling of a piece with no verified sale value remain among the open decisions below.

Hewge does not hold the resale price. Follow the fee, acceptance and approval process in [permanent provenance](permanent-provenance.txt); coordinate the external sale and physical handover around it.

## Decisions still open at the handovers

### Gift registration and surprise

Does the purchaser become the initial registered owner, or can the recipient be registered directly after acceptance? When can Hewge contact the recipient without spoiling the gift? Who handles a declined gift or a fit problem? Does an initial purchase-as-gift carry a transfer fee, or is that reserved for subsequent transfers? A founding gift with no verified sale value also needs an agreed basis for any later gift-transfer fee.

### Creative and technical decision rights

When artistic intent, design, wearability and budget conflict, who has the final decision on each? What constitutes an included revision, a new direction or grounds to stop? Who signs off the finished construction and care instructions? Which observations can the producer resolve independently, and which require the artist, designer or founder to approve a change?

### Production deviations and replacement

What happens if making or final inspection reveals a problem that changes the agreed result? Decide how rework, additional cost and timing are approved. If an original is irreparably damaged during production, what can be remade, what must be recorded and how is the one-of-one commitment maintained? Clarify how tests and samples are handled separately from the saleable original.

### The exact scope of uniqueness and authorship

Each garment is one of one. What additional promises attach to its artwork, pattern or design? Can the artist use related work in another medium? What rights do collaborators have to publish process material, and what is the purchaser receiving beyond the physical piece? How will the producer's contribution be credited where roles overlap or specialist work is shared?

### The boundary of mystery

Hewge administrators design and decide the mystery separately for each release. Record what the audience knows, what a bidder knows when committing, what the custodian learns later and when promised reveals occur. Artist identity may be part of a later reveal where the approved terms and supported flow permit it. Application support requires artist/material details at opening; a different plan needs disclosure review and tested implementation before use. Follow [the administrator playbook](administrator-playbook.md), including the response to delays or a reveal differing from the agreed description.

### The complete economics of the collaboration

Are collaborators paid fixed fees, a share of the original sale or a combination? Does any participation continue on resale? How are gifted commissions, editorial production, sampling and ongoing support funded? These choices affect both the commission agreement and the minimum viable sale price. Define who carries agreed development costs if a piece does not reach release.

### Resale settlement and physical handover

The transfer requires verified recipient acceptance and possession evidence, Hewge inspection of identity and entitlement, confirmed recipient-paid fee and final conditional approval. The fee is 0.5% of resale value; subsequent gifts use the last verified sale value. Hewge does not hold the garment resale price. Define the buyer–seller payment and delivery sequence around those steps, who bears risk between them, and the recovery path if condition or entitlement checks fail. Follow the fee-refund procedure in permanent-provenance.txt.

### The limits of continuing care

What fitting, repair, restoration and authentication support accompanies the piece, for how long and at whose cost? Which alterations remain compatible with its recorded identity? What happens when a tag or certificate is lost while the physical work remains legitimate? What support remains available if the original producer or another collaborator can no longer undertake it?

### Recognition across different people and transactions

The public record distinguishes creator, original purchaser, giver, current custodian and former custodians where relevant and permitted. Decide which relationships are shown and whose consent governs each. Use a role-appropriate anonymous description when a person does not opt into public identification.
